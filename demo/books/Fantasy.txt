Chapter 1: The Lamplighter's Debt

Every lamp in Harrowgate had a name, and Wren knew all four hundred of them. She lit them at dusk with a brass pole and a word her grandmother had taught her, and she put them out at dawn with the same word spoken backwards. The city believed the lamps ran on oil. The lamps knew better, and so did Wren.

On the night the debt came due, the lamp on Cinder Lane refused to light. She said the word twice, then a third time, louder, until a window opened above her and an old man told her to hush. The wick stayed dark. Wren set down her pole and pressed her palm to the cold glass, the way you would feel a child's forehead for a fever.

"You owe us a name," the lamp said. Its voice was the sound of a match before it catches. "Your grandmother promised one, a long time ago. We have been patient."

Wren had heard the story, of course. Everyone in her family had: how her grandmother, young and reckless, had bargained with the lamps for a light that would never go out, and how the price had been left unpaid, rolling quietly down the years like a coin under a table. She had always thought it was the kind of story you told to keep children away from open flames.

Chapter 2: A Name for the Dark

The lamps would not take just any name. Wren tried her own first, because it seemed fair, but the flame only flickered and lay down again, unimpressed. She tried the names of streets and rivers, of a cat she had loved, of a king nobody remembered. By midnight the whole of Cinder Lane had gone dark, one lamp after another, as if the street were closing its eyes.

It was the old man from the window who found her, sitting on the kerb with her pole across her knees. He brought two cups of tea and a blanket that smelled of cedar, and he did not ask what she was doing, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for her in a long time.

"My wife used to light these," he said at last. "Before you. Before your grandmother, even. She said they were lonely, not hungry. People mistake the one for the other."

Wren looked at the dark lamps, the long row of them leaning toward each other in the cold. Lonely, not hungry. She stood, lifted her pole, and instead of a name she began to tell them a story: the one about a girl who learned four hundred names so that no light in the city would ever have to wait to be called.
