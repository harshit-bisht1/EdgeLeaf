Chapter 1: The Printer's Apprentice

London, in the winter of 1666, still smelled of smoke. Thomas Whitlock was twelve years old and had lost his mother, his house and both his shoes to the Great Fire, and he considered himself lucky, because he had not lost his hands, and a boy with hands could learn a trade. He found his on Fleet Street, in a print shop with half a roof.

Master Jonas Pell was a tall, stooped man with ink in the creases of his knuckles that no amount of scrubbing would shift. He looked at Thomas for a long moment, then held out a composing stick and a tray of type. "Set your name," he said. "Backwards. Then we'll see."

Thomas had never read a word in his life. But he had watched the shop through the broken window for three days, and he had seen how the letters were picked, which way they faced, how the apprentice's fingers moved. He set T, then H, then O, slowly, feeling the cold lead shapes, mirror-wise and upside down.

When he had finished, Master Pell inked the line, pressed a scrap of paper to it, and held it up to the grey light. THOMAS, it read, a little crooked, every letter the right way round. The printer grunted, which Thomas would later learn was the closest he ever came to praise, and showed him where he would sleep.

Chapter 2: Words for the City

That spring the city began to rebuild, and every builder, every merchant and every parish needed notices, bills and broadsides, and the shop on Fleet Street worked by candlelight long after the bells. Thomas learned to read the way he had learned to set type: backwards first, then forwards, the letters becoming words and the words becoming the sound of other people thinking.

He read proclamations about new streets of brick and stone. He read the lists of the missing, posted on the shop's door, and he read them every morning, searching for one name, until the morning Master Pell quietly took them down.

"Some words are for printing," the old man said, "and some are for keeping." He gave Thomas a blank sheet, the good paper, the kind they saved for gentlemen. "Set one for keeping."

Thomas worked on it all evening. When the ink was dry he folded the page small and kept it inside his shirt, against his heart, where it stayed for the rest of his life: four lines about a woman who had sung while she baked bread on Pudding Lane, so that somebody, somewhere, would always know her name.
